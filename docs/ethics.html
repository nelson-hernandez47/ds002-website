The AI Gaydar Study

Author

Nelson Hernandez

Published

November 18, 2026

The scenario

In September 2017, two researchers at Stanford’s Graduate School of Business, Yilun Wang and Michal Kosinski, released a study in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology in 2018 claiming that a computer could look at a photo of someone’s face and guess whether that person is gay or straight, and that it could do this more accurately than people can (Wang & Kosinski, 2018).

The data science component

Everything in this subsection comes from the paper (Wang & Kosinski, 2018). The researchers collected photos from public profiles on a U.S. dating website, which the paper does not name. The researchers also took each person’s sexual orientation from their profile, based on the gender of the partners the person said they were looking for. They kept only photos showing one clear face looking at the camera, and they paid workers on Amazon’s Mechanical Turk to confirm that each face belonged to an adult, was white, and matched the gender on the profile. The final sample was 35,326 photos of 14,776 people aged 18 to 40, with equal numbers of gay and straight men (3,947 each) and of lesbian and straight women (3,441 each).

Each photo was then run through VGG-Face, an existing neural network trained to recognize faces, which turns a face into a list of 4,096 numbers. The researchers reduced those numbers to 500 with singular value decomposition, used a LASSO logistic regression to predict sexual orientation from them, and tested the model with 20-fold cross-validation, so it was always graded on people it had not been trained on.

The paper reports accuracy as AUC, meaning that if the model is given one gay person and one straight person, the AUC is how often it ranks the gay person as more likely to be gay. With one photo per person, the AUC was 0.81 for men and 0.71 for women. Furthermore, with five photos per person, it was 0.91 for men and 0.83 for women. On the other hand, human judges looking at the same photos reached only 0.61 for men and 0.54 for women.

The ethical dilemma

The authors actually seemingly presented the study as a warning. Photos of billions of people already sit on dating sites, photo-sharing sites, and government databases, so ordinary machine learning tools like theirs “could be deployed to detect sexual orientation without a person’s consent or knowledge” (Wang & Kosinski, 2018). GLAAD and the Human Rights Campaign (HRC), two of the largest LGBTQ advocacy groups in the U.S., called the study dangerous and flawed, and warned that a repressive government could use research like this to identify and persecute people it believes are gay (GLAAD & HRC, 2017). The dilemma is whether it is ethical to build and publish a working method for exposing a private, sometimes criminalized trait, using the faces of thousands of people who never agreed to be studied, in order to warn the world that such a method is possible.

Four ethical questions

1. What is the permission structure for using the data? Was it followed?

A permission structure is the set of approvals that allow researchers to use data, such as review by an ethics board and the terms of the platform the data came from. In this study, the permission structure was followed on paper. The paper states that the study was approved by Stanford’s Institutional Review Board (IRB), the committee that reviews research on human subjects (Wang & Kosinski, 2018), and the American Psychological Association, which publishes the journal, reviewed the researchers’ documentation of that approval before the paper came out (Venton, 2017). However, Metcalf (2017) argues that this approval says little about whether the research was ethical. In his reading, the IRB most likely treated the study as low risk because the photos were already public and the researchers never interacted with anyone, and the federal rules that IRBs enforce were written for studies that intervene in people’s lives, not for studies that mine data that already exists. He also notes that IRBs are not allowed to weigh harms to people outside the study (Metcalf, 2017). Unfortunately, the other part of the permission structure, the dating site’s terms of service, cannot be checked, because the paper does not name the site as previously discussed.

2. What was the consent structure for recruiting participants? Were the participants aware of the ways their data would be used for research? Was informed consent possible? Can you provide informed consent for applications that are yet foreseen?

Informed consent means that people know what data is being collected about them, how it will be used, what the risks are, and that they agree before anything happens. This study had no consent structure, because no one was recruited. Instead, the researchers collected photos that 14,776 people had posted to find a date. Those people were not told that their photos were being used for research, so they could not have been aware of how their data would be used. Informed consent was possible in principle, since researchers can recruit volunteers who agree to share their photos and sexual orientation, but collecting existing profiles meant that no one was asked. The study also shows why consent cannot cover applications that are not yet foreseen, asomeone filling out a dating profile could not have imagined that their face would help train a classifier for their own sexuality, so even agreeing to the site’s terms would not have been informed consent to this use.

3. What was the data collection process? Were the observations collected ethically? Are there missing observations?

The data were collected by downloading public dating-site photos, filtering them, and having Mechanical Turk workers judge each person’s age, race, and gender (Wang & Kosinski, 2018). The observations were collected without the knowledge of the people in them, and other parts of the process also raise ethical concerns. The label “gay” or “straight” was not a person’s own description of their identity but the gender they were searching for on the site. GLAAD and HRC (2017) pointed out that this treats sexual activity and sexual orientation as the same thing, that the researchers took people’s ages and orientations from their profiles without verifying them, and that the study allowed for only two orientations, which is unlikely tru for every case. Another concern was that race was not self-reported either. Strangers decided whether each face looked white, and Metcalf (2017) notes that the workers’ instructions used example photos to show them what each racial category was supposed to look like. There are many missing observations, and they are not random. The data contain no people of color, which the authors say is because the site had too few photos of non-white gay and lesbian users. There are also no bisexual people, no one outside the categories of man and woman, and no one over 40, and every gay or lesbian person in the data was out enough to post a public profile looking for same-gender partners, so closeted people are missing too (Wang & Kosinski, 2018; GLAAD & HRC, 2017).

4. Who was measured? Are those individuals representative of the people to whom we’d like to generalize / apply the algorithm? Should we analyze data if we do not know how the data were collected?

The people who were measured were young, white, out, American dating-site users whose race and gender were confirmed by strangers (Wang & Kosinski, 2018). They are not representative of the people a tool like this would be applied to: people in other countries, people of color, older people, bisexual people, people who have not come out, and much more. The authors concede that openly gay people may differ from people who are not open about it, so the classifier may be less accurate for them (Wang & Kosinski, 2018). They tested the model on a second sample of 918 photos of openly gay men from Facebook, where it separated those men from straight dating-site users 74% of the time, but those men were also openly gay (Wang & Kosinski, 2018). Even on people like the ones it was trained on, applying the model would label many people wrongly. In the paper’s own simulation of 1,000 men in which 70 were gay, 47 of the 100 men the model rated most likely to be gay actually were, so 53 of them were straight men flagged by mistake, and that was using five photos per person (Wang & Kosinski, 2018). GLAAD and HRC (2017) raised this exact concern, too! If a tool like this were used to target people, straight people would be wrongly labeled too. As for analyzing data without knowing how it was collected, the researchers knew their own process, but readers cannot fully check it, because the website the data came from is not named (Wang & Kosinski, 2018).

Why it matters

Who benefits? The researchers gained a high-profile publication, and the method is useful to anyone who wants to sort people by sexual orientation without asking them. The authors argue that LGBTQ people benefit too, from the warning and from evidence that sexual orientation is not a choice (Wang & Kosinski, 2018).

Who is neglected or harmed? The 14,776 people whose faces were used without their knowledge are harmed. So are gay people living where homosexuality is criminalized or persecuted, who are the people GLAAD and HRC (2017) and Metcalf (2017) worried about. Metcalf points to Chechnya, where the government was targeting gay men, as the kind of place where an automated “gaydar” would be used, and straight people who would be misclassified are harmed as well. Queer people of color, bisexual people, and people outside the gender binary are neglected as well because they were left out of the data, but they would not be left out of the tool’s use.

Were the ethical violations in the interest of profit, surveillance, or power? Not profit, since no product was sold. However, for surveillance, yes. Whatever the intent, the paper shows how members of a minority group could be identified from photos without their consent or knowledge, which the authors themselves warn that companies and governments could do (Wang & Kosinski, 2018). Concerning power, the researchers decided who counts as gay, white, a man, or a woman for thousands of people who had no say, and then published a method that lets anyone with enough photos do the same.
